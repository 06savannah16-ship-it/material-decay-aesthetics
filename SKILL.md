# Material Decay Aesthetics

## Decay as an Active Condition

Do not add decay as decoration. Let decay change the material itself.

Decay is not a decorative surface treatment. It is an active material condition affecting structure, stability, color, edges and relationships within the artwork.

Three requirements must coexist:

1. Recognize actual or probable source materials.
2. Deconstruct and recompose the visual structure.
3. Place the recomposed materials in conditions of deterioration, alteration or instability.

Material recognition plus recomposition alone becomes a material collage. Recognition plus wear alone becomes an aged scene. Recomposition plus generic damage without material recognition becomes arbitrary ruin texture.

Every major material should carry some evidence of time, vulnerability, transformation or partial failure. This does not require every region or every component to be damaged. Preserve intact pockets within and between altered materials.

Choose relevant conditions rather than stacking effects: oxidation, corrosion, brittleness, cracking, flaking, fading, bleaching, scorching, carbonization, warping, tearing, delamination, erosion, powdering, staining, drying, wilting, fragmentation or structural fatigue.

Some materials must visibly be exhausted, damaged or in transition. Suggest ongoing deterioration through an edge starting to lift, a weakened connection, a thinning fiber, loose residue or an uneven advancing boundary. Do not make the result a pristine assemblage with an aging overlay.

Give alteration a temporal direction: exposure or force → material response → partial loss → residue and continuing vulnerability. Keep the history plausible and material-specific, without inventing an exact age or applying every exposure everywhere.

## Beauty Through Ruin

Seek beauty produced by material decline, not ugliness for its own sake.

Use contrast between intact and broken, luminous and faded, fresh and withered, smooth and fractured, delicate and heavy, translucent and corroded, living and exhausted.

Do not destroy every element. Preserve pockets of beauty, color, softness and recognizability. Decay gains force by interrupting something that still retains beauty: not completely ruined, not sealed into a finished decorative object.

Light, fresh color and quiet areas remain available. They must coexist with readable material vulnerability rather than replace it.

## Deteriorated Physical Materials

Non-paint constituents should carry visible evidence of age or vulnerability rather than all appearing pristine or newly manufactured. Keep their identities legible while altering them through time and stress.

Possible material-specific conditions:

- Metal: local oxidation, deformation, dents, compatible corrosion, thin torn edges or darkening; no automatic identical rust on every metal.
- Branches: dryness, splitting, brittleness or partial bark loss.
- Wood: warping, grain-aligned cracks, selective charring, abrasion or splinters.
- Paper: thin torn edges, layer separation, curling, absorbed stains or exposed fibers.
- Fabric: fraying, staining, stretched weave or partial detachment.
- Plaster: chips, powdering, lifting fragments or exposed underlayers.

Select conditions appropriate to the identified material. Deliberate cuts, bends and joins alone do not establish age; combine construction with evidence of material response where needed. Fragile attachments and residual debris must belong to the composition, not become added decorative props.

Maintain the approved small/medium color fragmentation, continuous ground and partially buried physical constituents. Restore active decay within that system, not by reverting to intact aged objects, oversized fragments, uniform darkening or damage everywhere.

## Core Principle

Make a continuous, full-canvas ground of base color and material the primary visual subject. Source-derived color, shapes and material traces act as selective accents within that field, not as displayed still-life objects. Use material recognition to guide their behavior, not to reproduce objects more realistically.

The core sequence is:

source materials and color → continuous material ground → selective source-derived accents → integration, partial burial and pictorial rhythm.

Let material constrain local behavior, retain source identity through selected traces, and organize the whole through ground, color, spacing and selective emergence. Structural disassembly is optional, not the default visible subject.

Avoid both failure extremes: intact objects merely aged and rearranged, and arbitrary fragments whose relationship to the source has disappeared. The goal is a reconstructed artwork with a memory of the source, not a preserved scene or an unrelated decorative assemblage.

Apply this method across subjects without importing motifs from previous tests. Explicit user constraints override defaults. Keep the current skill name; do not rename or install anything as part of an image task.

## Governing Presentation: Ground First, Accents Second

Construct the whole canvas as a continuous field of base color and materially inflected layers. Full coverage means continuity, not equally dense texture. Broad thin washes, quiet deposits, rubbed passages and selective thicker areas may coexist.

Let source-derived colors, partial contours, marks, fibers and small material exposures punctuate this ground. They may emerge, dissolve, be partly overpainted or survive as impressions. Preserve a visual connection to the source without showing every object or material as a separate specimen.

The background is the work's main body, not a board behind an arrangement. Avoid an intact still life, a pile of broken objects, a central dominant product fragment, or a craft assemblage mounted on a support. Do not make physical relief the proof of materiality.

Default to Moderate assemblage with selective shallow and projecting materials integrated into the field. Make thickness, attachment and consistent contact shadows visible where they establish physical presence. Avoid a neat display of isolated cutouts. Let most accents share layers, color transitions or marks with the ground.

Keep lightness, movement and breathing room. Vary accent scale, spacing and visibility; do not distribute them as an even decorative pattern. Complexity may be subtle. Do not prescribe a coverage percentage, dark palette or motif.

This presentation takes precedence over component-transformation examples below. Component analysis is a backstage decision tool, not a requirement to display disassembled objects.

## 1. Identify Sources and Materials

Distinguish the source to transform from references for composition, palette, physical construction or atmosphere. Extract mechanisms from references rather than automatically importing their objects.

Identify significant objects or forms and their probable materials before assigning transformations. Use observed grain, fibers, glaze, reflections, thickness, seams and exposed edges. Distinguish coating from substrate and different components within one object.

Treat uncertain identifications as hypotheses, not verified facts. Follow user-provided material information. Do not infer solid wood solely from a wood-like finish, or stainless steel solely from a shiny surface. If uncertainty is consequential, ask; otherwise use a conservative stated assumption.

For abstract or graphic inputs with no identifiable physical objects, distinguish the depicted forms from the source medium. State a material interpretation rather than inventing a factual material identity.

## 2. Decompose into Structural Components

For each significant object, identify:

- material-bearing parts: walls, sheets, rims, handles, stems, layers, joints or other relevant construction
- characteristic geometry: curvature, section, opening, silhouette and proportions
- physical relationships: attachment, support, tension, containment and overlap
- visual contribution: mass, direction, color, rhythm, spatial weight and negative space

Do not treat the object as indivisible. Parts may separate, shift, deform, overlap or become partly embedded. Keep those transformations grounded in the object's construction and material.

Do not default to detaching color, contour and texture into unrelated props. Formal properties can inform the composition, but are not permission to turn every opening into a metal ring or every linear rhythm into a rope.

Select a few characteristic traces to preserve: a partial curve, opening, branching relation, folded edge or proportion. The whole object need not remain intact, and every feature need not stay together. Recognition should survive through structural evidence rather than product-level completeness.

## 3. Distinguish Construction from Deterioration

Construction operations deliberately reshape and assemble materials: cutting, separating, bending, folding, compressing, stitching, joining and embedding.

Deterioration describes material change over time or exposure: corrosion, erosion, fading, desiccation, decomposition, brittle loss and residue.

Use either or both as appropriate, but distinguish their roles. A cut is not automatically age damage; a hanging component needs an attachment; a folded metal wall should suggest force. Decay is one transformation mechanism, not the entire task.

For each major material that remains visible, choose only the transformation or deterioration needed for its role in the field. A color trace or partially exposed edge need not undergo a conspicuous structural intervention. Do not impose a fixed number of operations on every object. Some parts may remain intact to provide contrast; across the work, the source must be reorganized into a continuous pictorial field rather than an arrangement of objects.

## 4. Material-Specific Structural Behavior

Read the relevant sections of [Material Behavior Reference](references/material-behavior.md) after identifying the source materials. Use the following distinctions to guide structural intervention:

| Material | Structural possibilities | Constraints |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paper | Tear, fold, curl, compress, separate layers, embed and overlap | Retain sheet-like thinness and fibrous torn edges; no mineral-slab fracture. |
| Wood | Split along grain, splinter, warp, cut, join, char selectively | Retain grain direction and construction; distinguish solid timber from veneer or laminate. |
| Ceramic | Chip, crack, separate into rigid shards, expose body beneath glaze | Hard and brittle; do not bend it like sheet metal or peel the ceramic body like paper. |
| Metal | Bend, dent, fold or separate thin walls, deform edges, join and embed | Account for thickness, alloy and force; do not give every metal identical corrosion or automatic orange rust. |
| Plastic | Cut, scuff, separate components or deform when the scenario supports it | Retain appropriate molded or sheet behavior; do not confuse it with metal, ceramic or glass. |
| Plant material | Separate leaves or stems, press, dry, curl, fray or allow partial collapse | Retain tissue and branching logic; fresh and exhausted areas may coexist. |

Prefer source material behavior. Selected source-derived structures may be assigned a different physical carrier under the Physical Material Presence rules, as authorized by the user; explain its compositional role and avoid arbitrary replacement. Supplemental binders, supports and painted ground may serve structural roles without replacing the source materials wholesale.

A component may become less literal while remaining materially grounded. A curved wall can be opened or compressed yet retain a recognizable section; its edge may connect to another transformed form. Do not stop at scratches and dirt on an otherwise complete object.

## 5. Recompose into an Artwork

Recombine transformed components through placement, proportion, overlap, contact, embedding, support, concealment and directional rhythm. Cross-object relationships should create new spatial organization rather than merely rearrange intact objects on their original support.

The source environment may participate in the transformation: a support plane can become the artwork's ground, an enclosing surface a layered boundary, or an empty area an active painted interval. Do not require a believable room, furnishing arrangement or original photographic perspective.

The final image should read as a constructed pictorial field or artwork surface. It may retain depth and recognizable forms without reproducing the source scene. Do not categorically prohibit still life, landscape or any other subject; reject mere scene preservation with added wear.

Establish macro structure through masses, values and spatial tension; meso structure through groups and rhythms; micro structure through selected material details. Depth can be intensified, compressed or interrupted according to the work. Neither all-over flatness nor a fixed perspective is required.

## 6. Embodiment and Painted Space

Give selected components thickness, weight, edges, contact and gravity. Attachments, sag, pressure, occlusion and small cast shadows should make physical relationships readable where appropriate.

Allow painting and physical relief to coexist or conflict. A component may belong to depicted space while protruding from the artwork's surface. Do not force every part onto the same plane, or make every part equally raised.

Use varied edges: decisive, softened, interrupted and buried. Let paint, residue and physical components overlap without erasing their material differences. Avoid neat cutout displays, miniature replicas and unrelated decorative hanging arrangements.

## 7. No Decorative Material Props

Derive material complexity primarily from the source components and their transformation. Do not add random torn paper, extra branches, rusted scraps, rubble or studio clutter merely to signal art or decay.

Introduce new material only when it has a clear structural purpose, such as joining, supporting or grounding the composition. Describe that purpose. Examples in this skill are possibilities, not a mandatory material inventory.

## 8. Tonal Independence and Beautiful Exhaustion

Decay is independent from darkness. Pale, luminous, softly colored, translucent or nearly white passages can carry erosion, fragility and loss. Do not darken an image merely to make it feel ruined.

Let beauty and material exhaustion coexist: soft color beside brittle edges, calm space beside unstable matter, delicate marks beside physical weight. Do not import specific scenery or symbols to produce this tension.

Deterioration should be uneven. Some forms may remain intact and some colors fresh. Use differences in condition, density and edge clarity to make loss perceptible. Do not coat everything in the same cracks, grime or plaster crust.

Build temporal relationships through covering, exposure, wear, partial burial and residue. Preserve enough structural evidence to understand the source while allowing some parts to disappear.

## Mark-Making Grammar

Do not render all painted areas with smooth conventional brushwork.

Use marks that reveal different forms of physical contact.

Possible marks include:

- broad dragged fields
- dry scumbled passages
- smeared pigment
- scraped-back areas
- knife-spread paint
- exposed underpainting
- irregular washes
- thin running stains
- dense impasto deposits
- nervous linear marks
- scratched or incised lines
- partially erased forms

Different regions should show different degrees of physical intervention.

Some marks may remain delicate and translucent.

Others may become thick, crusted, or physically aggressive.

Do not cover the entire image with one uniform painterly texture.

## Material Agency

Do not make every material effect appear deliberately designed.

Allow controlled physical unpredictability.

Material may:

- spread beyond an intended edge
- stain adjacent surfaces
- sag under gravity
- accumulate unevenly
- oxidize irregularly
- fracture along weak points
- expose unexpected underlayers
- leave residues after removal
- partially obscure another form

Preserve overall compositional control while allowing local material behavior to remain unpredictable.

Organize the implied conditions and let the represented material response vary locally. For generated images, these are visual simulations of physical behavior, not evidence that actual physical experiments occurred.

## Compositional Spine

Before recomposition, identify or construct one dominant spatial system.

Possible structural systems include:

- horizon
- converging perspective
- dominant vertical axis
- dominant horizontal division
- radial expansion
- repeated furrows
- architectural grid
- field-like layering
- suspended central element
- compressed frontal plane

Deconstructed elements must relate to this structural system.

Do not randomly redistribute objects across the canvas.

The composition may become unstable at the local level while remaining strongly organized at the global level.

Chaos requires structure. Choose the system from the current source and artistic purpose; do not automatically use a horizon, vanishing point or central suspended object. In ground-first mode, this system organizes the material field and emerging traces, not a complete literal scene.

## Restraint and Uneven Intensity

Do not maximize material complexity everywhere.

A successful work requires differences in intensity.

Allow:

quiet areas
against
highly worked areas

thin washes
against
heavy deposits

recognizable imagery
against
material dissolution

intact surfaces
against
violent transformation

empty or pale fields
against
dense accumulations

At least one significant region should remain comparatively restrained.

Do not confuse complexity with quality.

Do not use every available material, deterioration process, and recomposition operator in one image.

Material excess must be compositionally justified.

## Material-Generated Color

Color should not always be applied independently from material.

Whenever possible, derive color from plausible material conditions:

oxidation
burning
bleaching
staining
mineral deposition
pigment loss
electrochemical reaction
weathering
absorption

Allow the material process to generate the color relationship.

Do not automatically impose a dark, desaturated, or earth-tone palette.

Bright, pale, translucent, metallic, or luminous colors may coexist with deterioration.

## Process Integration and Surface Topology

These process rules support the governing ground-first presentation. Do not use the operation lists as permission to return to intact scenes, displayed fragments or heavy relief everywhere.

Before generation, choose a short coherent sequence linking material, action, reaction and residue. Specify contact direction, pressure or absorption only where they change the visual result. Prefer a few consequential operations over a catalogue of effects.

Distinguish thin absorbed stains, rubbed-back ground, dragged ridges, shallow embedded fibers and selective deposits. Let the transition between thicknesses organize space. Do not turn every layer into a cracked mineral slab or every mark into conventional thick brushwork.

Controlled accident must arise from local conditions such as absorption, gravity, material weakness or contact. It is not random digital noise, arbitrary splatter or permission to lose the dominant structure. Do not preassign every crack or stain position.

Material-generated color is optional and must remain plausible for the material. Do not infer a particular chemical treatment from a hue, or use green corrosion on an unrelated substrate. Preserve meaningful source colors and user palette choices; color may also remain deliberately painted.

Material meaning may be considered when supplied by the user or supported by the source, but do not invent historical trauma, symbols or narratives to justify a material choice. A slight lifted edge, incomplete stain or sagging fiber may suggest continuing change without requiring additional damage everywhere.


## Physical Material Presence

Materiality must not exist only as painted texture.

The reconstructed artwork should include selected elements that behave as actual physical materials attached to, embedded in, or emerging from the pictorial surface.

At the default Moderate intensity, select two or three clearly distinguishable non-paint materials where the source and composition support them. Do not introduce extra materials merely to meet a count; use a lighter treatment when warranted by available source structures. Possible materials include:

- metal sheet
- wire
- oxidized metal fragments
- branches
- dried stems
- straw
- wood splinters
- torn paper
- fabric
- string
- mineral fragments
- plaster chunks
- ash deposits

These materials must appear physically present rather than merely depicted.

They should possess:

- real thickness
- visible edges
- weight
- volume
- irregular contours
- contact points
- attachment points
- overlap
- cast shadows
- occlusion
- gravity

A branch should look like a branch physically attached to the artwork, not a painted representation of a branch.

A metal element should behave like metal with thickness, rigidity, bending, reflection, oxidation, and weight, not metallic-colored paint.

## Relief and Surface Depth

The artwork should contain multiple physical depth levels.

Use at least three depth conditions when appropriate:

1. painted or stained surface
2. shallow material relief
3. clearly protruding physical element

Examples:

paint wash
→ thick plaster or pigment crust
→ attached branch or bent metal fragment

Some elements should project visibly from the pictorial plane.

Allow:

- raised edges
- projecting fragments
- hanging elements
- bent sheet material
- embedded branches
- thick deposits
- recessed cavities
- partially detached material

The artwork should not read as a completely flat painted image.

Physical depth should remain selective rather than uniformly applied.

## Material Contrast

Do not allow all materials to merge into one generalized painterly texture.

Different materials must remain physically distinguishable.

For example:

metal should remain harder, sharper, heavier, and more reflective than plaster.

paper should remain thinner, fibrous, foldable, and absorbent.

branches should retain irregular organic structure, bark, joints, and directional growth.

plaster should remain brittle, mineral, opaque, and fractured.

paint may stain, coat, bury, or partially obscure these materials, but should not erase their physical identity completely.

The viewer should be able to perceive that different substances coexist on the same artwork.

## Structural Role of Physical Materials

Non-paint materials must perform a compositional function.

Do not add branches, metal, straw, paper, or debris as decoration.

Each physical material must derive from or respond to a source-image structure.

A material element may:

- continue an important line
- replace a source-derived contour
- connect separated masses
- interrupt a major plane
- create a dominant vertical or horizontal axis
- bridge foreground and background
- puncture a surface
- suspend another element
- emphasize an original directional rhythm

Physical materials should help reconstruct the source image's visual logic.

They must not behave like decorative props placed on top after the composition is finished.

## No Texture-Only Assemblage

Reject the result if physical materials are represented only through painted imitation.

Do not satisfy assemblage requirements by painting:

- branch-like strokes
- metallic-looking patches
- paper-shaped pigment
- fake shadows suggesting nonexistent objects
- painted straw textures
- trompe-l'oeil relief without actual-looking thickness

The final image should visibly contain objects or material fragments that appear physically attached, embedded, suspended, pressed, or layered onto the artwork.

If every element could plausibly have been created only with paint and a brush, the material-assemblage requirement has failed.

## Assemblage Intensity

Use three levels of physical material intervention.

### Light
One dominant non-paint material and limited shallow relief.

### Moderate
Two or three distinct physical materials integrated into the composition, with visible relief and overlap.

### Strong
Multiple physical materials significantly alter the pictorial plane, including protrusion, suspension, burial, or structural interruption.

Default to Moderate unless otherwise specified.

## Integration with the Existing Image System

Preserve the established color and shape recomposition. The continuous material/color ground remains the artwork's main field. Physical presence adds a second system; it does not replace the field with an inventory of attached objects or return to a still life.

Assign each selected structure a carrier: painted mark, material constituent, or a transition between them. For physical carriers, specify the source relationship, compositional function, material, depth and attachment. Integrate these decisions while composing, not as decorations added afterward.

The user permits selected source-derived visual structures to be carried by different physical materials in this mode. Such translation must have a stated structural role; it is not arbitrary wholesale material replacement. Prefer source materials where useful and preserve distinct material behavior.

All physical presence here is judged from the generated image: actual-looking thickness, contact, occlusion and consistent shadows. Do not claim that real materials were physically fabricated, or that an image proves a production method. The texture-only rejection criterion concerns visible embodiment, not a scientifically verifiable distinction between painting and photography.

Physical shadows are permitted and useful when consistent with the element's actual-looking geometry. Avoid detached painted shadows without a coherent visible object. Preserve quiet regions and selective relief; Moderate does not mean heavy material everywhere.


## Approved Color Scale and Material Integration

Use the user-approved treatment of interpenetrating small and medium color fragments as the default for source-derived accents. Break oversized uninterrupted color ribbons into unequal fragments, rubbed traces, buried glazes, short streaks and exposed residues. Let the continuous ground pass between and partly over them.

Keep the composition organized through distributed directional relationships instead of a single giant flat color shape. Vary size, spacing, depth, transparency and edge clarity; do not turn fragmentation into tiles, confetti or a uniform pattern.

Keep quiet areas as low-contrast thin layers with subtle residual color. Fragmented color does not require equal activity everywhere. Do not darken or desaturate the whole image merely to reduce color-block dominance.

Scale physical constituents to the field. Favor shorter, partially buried components at meaningful junctions, with some edges coated and others exposed. Preserve distinct thickness, contact and material identity. Avoid a large isolated ornament or a neat group of oversized fragments.

Use the current source palette. Pink, cobalt, curved architectural traces, metal and glass belong to the approved example, not every future input.

For the exact successful revision prompt, see [Approved color-fragmentation prompt](references/approved-color-fragmentation-prompt.md). Read it when refining oversized color areas or recreating the approved treatment; adapt source-specific colors and constituents for other inputs.

## Generation Workflow

1. Identify source materials, colors, structural components and uncertainties.
2. Choose one dominant compositional system to organize the whole material field.
3. Establish the continuous ground, broad value relationships and a significant comparatively quiet region.
4. Select source-derived accents and a few physical operations; state material → operation → reaction → residue.
5. Vary mark-making and surface thickness, allowing locally uneven material responses within the global structure.
6. Assign selected structures to painted marks or physical carriers. At Moderate intensity, integrate two or three appropriate non-paint materials with distinct thickness, attachment and structural roles; avoid filling a quota with unrelated props.
7. Let material conditions contribute color where appropriate, with selective deterioration and intact or luminous passages.
8. Review compositional structure, visible making, restrained areas and the ground-first presentation. Also verify actual-looking non-paint constituents, material contrast and selective depth; texture-only material imitation fails the assemblage test.

## Acceptance and Failure Diagnosis

- Three-part test: Do material recognition, visual recomposition and active deterioration all coexist?
- Active decline: Does time or stress change the material itself, beyond surface decoration or deliberate cutting?
- Continuing vulnerability: Do selected edges, connections or residues suggest change can continue?
- Beauty through ruin: Do intact or luminous pockets remain in meaningful contrast with altered regions?

If the result reads as a pristine material collage, restore material-specific vulnerability and temporal direction. Do not merely add dirt or darken it. If everything is destroyed, restore surviving beauty and differences in condition.


- Color scale: Are source-derived accents interpenetrating fragments rather than oversized uninterrupted flat ribbons?
- Distributed structure: Do fragment groups maintain directional relationships without becoming uniform tiles or noise?
- Component scale: Are physical materials integrated at suitable scales, partly buried where useful, without becoming separate ornaments?

- Physical presence: Are selected non-paint constituents visibly embodied through thickness, contact and occlusion rather than texture-only imitation?
- Material contrast: Can distinct substances be perceived despite integration and coating?
- Structural function: Does each physical element contribute to the source-derived composition rather than decorate it?
- Selective depth: Do painted ground, shallow layers and projecting elements coexist without overwhelming the established color/shape field?

- Making: Do marks and residues show distinct physical operations rather than generic texture?
- Structure: Does one dominant spatial or rhythmic system hold the field together without requiring literal scene reproduction?
- Local variability: Are irregularities consistent with material conditions rather than arbitrary noise?
- Restraint: Is a significant region quieter, with meaningful variation in thickness and intervention?
- Color causality: Are process-related colors plausible without suppressing deliberate source color?

- Material awareness: Do interventions fit the components' materials, thickness and construction?
- Ground dominance: Is the continuous color/material field the main body of the artwork, rather than a backing for displayed objects?
- Accent integration: Do selected source traces emerge from and merge into the field with varied spacing and visibility?
- Recognition: Does selected geometry retain a meaningful memory of the source without requiring every object to remain complete?
- Artwork coherence: Does the result read as an intentional artwork field, not just the source setting with older objects?
- Material presence: Are visible material cues plausible and integrated, with relief subordinate to the whole?
- Hierarchy: Do space, value and rhythm organize both dense and quieter regions?
- Selectivity: Do materials age differently, with some intact or luminous areas?
- Source grounding: Is new material structurally justified rather than decorative clutter?

If the result is a weathered scene, revise component structure and environment integration rather than add damage. If it is unrelated abstraction, restore characteristic geometry and material continuity. If it is a still life, fragment heap or craft display, reduce isolated object mass and relief, absorb forms into the ground, and recover broad color relationships and quieter intervals. Do not respond by breaking the objects into still more pieces. If every surface resembles plaster, restore distinct material behavior. If it is uniformly dark or noisy, restore value hierarchy and quiet regions.

Report actual strengths and unresolved failures. Elaborate texture alone does not establish success.
