# Material Behavior Reference

Read relevant sections after identifying source materials and structural components. Use this reference for material behavior, not as permission for arbitrary substitution. The main skill governs structural transformation, partial recognition and composition. Distinguish deliberate construction from deterioration; compatible supporting processes are optional, not a global damage quota.

## Material Categories

### Paper

Possible deterioration:

- foxing
- water staining
- tide lines
- fiber breakage
- edge loss
- discoloration
- abrasion
- delamination
- folding damage
- faded ink
- mold staining

Paper should retain visible fibers, layered edges, softness, compression, tearing, or absorption behavior.

Avoid making paper look like stone or metal.

### Metal

Possible deterioration:

- oxidation
- corrosion
- pitting
- rust
- verdigris
- flaking
- mineral accretion
- staining
- surface abrasion

Metal deterioration should follow plausible chemical or environmental behavior.

Oxidation should develop unevenly around edges, recesses, joints, moisture zones, scratches, or exposed areas.

Do not apply rust uniformly over the entire surface.

### Painted Surface

Possible deterioration:

- craquelure
- lifting
- flaking
- pigment loss
- exposed ground layer
- abrasion
- blanching
- fading
- surface deposits
- delamination

Cracks should follow material stress rather than appear as a random crack overlay.

Losses should reveal underlying layers.

The image should suggest that the painted surface has thickness and stratigraphy.

### Plaster, Stone, and Architectural Surface

Possible deterioration:

- cracking
- erosion
- spalling
- powdering
- salt efflorescence
- exposed aggregate
- staining
- abrasion
- water damage
- structural fracture

Architectural surfaces should feel heavy, mineral, porous, layered, and physically vulnerable.

Damage should relate to gravity, water movement, stress, weather exposure, or material weakness.

### Organic Materials: Leaves, Flowers, Wood, and Fruit

Use this category for plant-derived subjects. Paper remains governed by the Paper category; do not treat every cellulose-based material as living plant tissue.

Interpret material and condition visually. Describe uncertain causes as an artistic scenario, not a diagnosis of species, organisms, disease, or an exact elapsed time.

Before choosing a process, identify:

- the organ or tissue: leaf blade and veins, petal, stem, wood grain, bark, fruit skin and flesh
- its current state: fresh, withered, dry, already decomposing, or mixed
- the implied environment: dry and exposed, sheltered, damp, or in contact with wet ground
- which structures should persist as softer tissue is lost

Choose a coherent environmental route. Drying is not the same process as biological decomposition. Do not combine brittle dry surfaces and wet softened tissue in the same area without a visible spatial or temporal reason.

| Subject | Primary process options | Material-specific traces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leaves | Desiccation OR tissue decomposition | Drying produces curled, contracted blades and brittle margins; selective tissue loss may leave an interrupted vein network. Preserve leaf attachment and branching logic. |
| Flowers | Wilting/desiccation OR localized decomposition | Petals droop or contract according to their attachment, then become thin and brittle in a dry scenario; a damp scenario may instead produce localized soft collapse. Retain enough of the flower structure to identify it. |
| Wood | Fungal decay OR drying-related checking | Decay can expose weakened, friable or fibrous regions; drying checks follow grain and stress. Choose a coherent texture rather than combining every wood-decay pattern. |
| Fruit | Desiccation OR localized rot | Drying wrinkles skin and reduces volume; rot produces localized loss of support and sinking tissue, often around an existing damaged zone. Keep skin, flesh, stem and any exposed seeds structurally related. |

For each organic material, start with a primary process and add compatible supporting processes only when needed. Curling, shrinking, and color shifts can be consequences of the same drying process; they do not require separate effects.

Example selections:

- Dry leaf: primary = desiccation; optional secondary = brittle edge fragmentation.
- Fallen damp leaf: primary = selective tissue decomposition; preserve a partial vein structure rather than making every leaf a perfect lace skeleton.
- Flower: primary = wilting/desiccation; optional secondary = brittle petal-edge loss.
- Damp wood: primary = localized fungal decay; optional secondary = fragmentation of weakened material.
- Fruit: primary = localized rot; omit additional processes unless they clarify the same material history.

#### Organic Structure and Residue

Show change in volume, support, attachment and tissue integrity, not color alone. Where layers are exposed, use the subject's actual organization: skin and flesh, bark and wood, leaf tissue and veins. Do not impose plaster-like layers on petals or leaves.

Residual fragments should follow gravity, attachment, contact and the source material. Detached petals, leaf fragments or wood crumbs are optional consequences, not decorative clutter. Preserve sufficient original silhouette and structural landmarks at every intensity.

Keep intact and damaged regions unevenly distributed according to exposure and vulnerability. In a group of leaves or flowers, vary condition coherently; do not age every specimen by an identical amount.

Mold, moss, lichen and mushrooms are not interchangeable decay symbols. Do not add them by default. If visible fungal growth is explicitly selected, keep it localized and compatible with the chosen damp scenario rather than using a spotted overlay.

#### Organic Image Scale and Intensity

Judge changes at the supplied image scale. In a wide landscape, prioritize readable leaf posture, canopy density and a few coherent zones; do not invent macro-scale veins or pores visible from far away. Match detail to the scale of the final composition; reconstruction may change framing and scale.

- Light: slight loss of turgid shape or local tissue changes; most structure remains intact.
- Moderate: clearly altered posture, volume or localized tissue loss; characteristic anatomy remains legible.
- Severe: substantial collapse or fragmentation, with enough remaining veins, grain, skin or attachments to understand the former object.

Avoid uniform browning, identical holes, random black patches, blanket mold, melted-plastic softness, stone-like cracking on soft tissue, or a generic autumn color grade. Organic deterioration must be visible in structure as well as color.

### Water Environments: Neglected Ponds and Organic Accumulation

Treat water as an environment carrying suspended matter, organisms and residues, not as a solid surface that itself rots. Use this mode when the user requests neglected, stagnant-looking or organically degraded water. Do not infer contamination or a specific ecological diagnosis from a photograph.

For a water region, choose a coherent primary process and compatible supporting processes; avoid piling up unrelated effects. A useful pairing is organic matter accumulation/decomposition (primary) with localized algae or biofilm growth (secondary). Color, turbidity and disrupted reflections are consequences, not additional damage effects. In a preservation edit, preserve established deterioration unless the user asks to change it. In reconstruction mode, transform and recombine material-bearing components; do not default to material substitution.

#### Color, Texture and Spatial Distribution

- Derive uneven olive-brown, muted green or grey-brown water from the selected scenario and original lighting. Avoid recoloring the entire scene or treating every neglected pond as the same shade.
- Show suspended fine organic matter, softened submerged forms and occasional decomposing plant fragments. Do not substitute a digital noise overlay for water texture.
- Concentrate floating leaves, detritus and patchy films at sheltered edges, around supports and in plausible collection zones. Account for visible flow, wind and ripples instead of distributing debris evenly.
- Where abundant submerged growth is requested, use coherent aquatic plant or algal masses with depth and attachment cues. Combine dull green growth and brown decaying strands selectively; abundance alone is not evidence of decay.
- Distinguish surface film, suspended matter and submerged vegetation. They should occupy different depths and interrupt or obscure each other appropriately.
- Keep open water between patches unless near-total coverage is explicitly requested. Let exposed water carry ripples while thin films locally alter the surface texture. Do not turn water into opaque paint, tar or thick fantasy slime.

#### Transparency and Reflections

Translate informal requests such as "lower refraction" into the intended visible effect: reduced underwater visibility, more suspended matter, or reflection interrupted by surface films. Do not claim that neglect simply lowers the refractive index.

Control underwater visibility separately from surface reflection. Murky water can still reflect the sky and architecture; do not erase every reflection merely because the water is turbid.

As turbidity and depth increase, reduce submerged detail and contrast. In a strongly murky scenario, dense water plants should appear as partially obscured masses with only a few near-surface strands legible. Avoid an aquarium-like view of crisp stems throughout the foreground. Balance the user's request for abundant growth with believable optical concealment.

Keep reflection geometry aligned with the scene. Break reflections locally through ripples, films and floating fragments, rather than randomly smearing or bending the whole image. In preservation edits, retain original daylight unless requested otherwise. In reconstruction, use the final composition lighting consistently.

#### Water Intensity and Checks

- Light: a few sheltered detritus pockets, slight suspended matter and mostly open water.
- Moderate: uneven murk, localized films and substantial but partly concealed growth; recognizable reflections remain in open zones.
- Severe: low underwater visibility and extensive organic accumulation, while retaining spatial variation and enough cues to read the surface as water.

Before accepting an edit, check:

- Is the change visible in water texture and depth, not color alone?
- Are floating, suspended and submerged elements distinguishable?
- Is underwater clarity consistent with the chosen turbidity?
- Do accumulation zones follow the shore, supports and apparent water movement?
- Are remaining reflections geometrically plausible?
- Have unrelated garbage, dead animals, neon algae and horror effects been avoided unless requested?
